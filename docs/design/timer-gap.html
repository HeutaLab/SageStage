The timer gap

What Pat described, next to what Sage Stage actually ships — 2 August 2026. The right-hand widgets are rebuilt from the shipped markup, the shipped tokens and the vendored Lexend/Quicksand, frozen at the state I actually paused the running app at (00:08 of 1:00) — faithful to the code, but drawn on a neutral swatch, so your own stage background will differ. The red sound button is a byte-faithful reproduction of beep(4) from app.js:63 — the only sound our timers can make. (App-wide, Video and Embed do pipe third-party audio through the classroom speakers; the timers do not.)

Sample size: one. This is a single teacher after a single session on a phone. It is the first outside feedback the product has ever had, which makes it precious and non-representative at the same time. The 13 Aug testers may want something else entirely — so the last strip on this page is about what fits before then, not about everything that could be built.

The Visual timer is already in Pat's category — and it is a proper clock face: twelve ticks with the quarter marks drawn heavier, a wedge depleting clockwise from twelve, digits in a centre bubble that turn red at zero. It just has no costume.

Read on the picture: this is not a missing feature, it is a missing wardrobe. The mechanism Pat is asking for — a countdown you read as a shrinking shape rather than as numbers — shipped long ago and works well. What the egg, the candle and the sand timer add on top is character: a reason for a five-year-old to look up. One face becomes five, using the canvas that is already there.

The trade to name out loud: the wedge sits on a real clock dial, so it rehearses analogue clock-reading at exactly the age children are being taught it. Sand level and wax height read fraction-remaining beautifully and teach nothing about clocks. That makes faces an option alongside the disc, never a replacement for it — which is why the spec keeps the disc as the default and why the picker should probably say so.

2 · The sound — this is the real gap

Press the red button. That is not a sample of our timer's sound; that is the entire vocabulary of every sound Sage Stage generates — both timers, all six maths manipulatives and the noise meter, from one function. One sine wave at 880 Hz, repeated. A teacher can turn it off; they cannot change it, and there is no volume.
Level warning, so the demo isn't rigged: the red button plays at the app's real peak gain (0.4); the sketches beside it are quieter by design (≤0.25). Judge the character, not the loudness.

what we ship

One voice — but a chosen one

app.js:63 — a sine, not a square, with a shaped envelope: 20 ms attack, 0.35 s decay, four rings 0.45 s apart, and the audio context closed afterwards so nothing idles. Somebody picked that. And the timers are configurable in other ways — any duration to the second, plus Repeat for interval ringing at 10, 20, 30 minutes. The gap is no choice of sound, which is narrower and fairer than "no customisation".

Our entire sound paletteGentle enough for a reading corner. Useless for ending a tidy-up race on a high — and identical whether the lesson wanted calm or urgency.

And the one that cuts against us: this chime has never been verified inside the Tauri webview. The audio context is created outside a user gesture, so on the packaged desktop build the timer may currently make no sound at all. That spike is open in the spec (§13) and should be closed before any of the work below is scheduled — there is no point choosing between six sounds on a build that plays none.

3 · The countdown itself — a layer we simply don't have

Pat's favourite trick is the one with no equivalent in Sage Stage at all: music while the clock runs, getting faster as it empties. Not an alarm — atmosphere. Today our timers say nothing between Start and zero, then chime once. (Visually plenty happens — the teal bar depletes, the wedge steps every 200 ms — this is a hole in the audio layer only.)
Worth knowing: the site Pat linked appears to offer sound only as end-of-timer alarms too. The tension music is Pat supplying it themselves, off to one side of the timer — which is precisely why tying it to the clock is the interesting move rather than a catch-up.

The licensing catch, and the way round it. We can never ship Mission Impossible, Indiana Jones or Star Wars — those are protected compositions, and a close imitation infringes just as surely as the recording. Grieg died in 1907, so In the Hall of the Mountain King is ours to sequence ourselves — and it was literally written to accelerate. Because our tempo is driven by the clock, the chase can finish exactly when the time does. Pat's Spotify can't do that.

Can't ship, ever — and it isn't a failure

Pat named three film themes. None of them can go in the product: the Mission Impossible theme including its 5/4 ostinato, and the Williams scores, are protected compositions — a close imitation infringes as surely as the recording, and recordings carry a second rights layer on top. This row belongs here rather than in the deficiency column above: we are not behind on it, it is unbuildable. What is buildable lands the same classroom joke.

The summary in one line: of Pat's four asks, three are a costume change on machinery that already works, and one — the sound — is a genuine hole worth a day — and the decision this page exists to make is slice 1, before you go away: the literal ask, one session, both timers improved, and nothing changed for anyone who never opens the gear. Everything else waits for 13 Aug, when the sample size stops being one.

The most valuable thing in those seven messages isn't a feature at all. A working teacher opened this on a phone, unprompted, and said it replaces smarts and has way more features — then immediately started asking for polish rather than for fixes. That is the review you want, and it says the timers are right, not wrong.
